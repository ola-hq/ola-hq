He wanted it all — Life gave him something else.

Marty Supreme arrived with a ton of hype, and honestly, it mostly earns it. It’s a long movie, but it stays engaging the entire time, constantly moving, constantly connecting dots. Nothing feels wasted. By the end, everything is tied together cleanly, which is rare for a film this ambitious.

At the center is Marty himself — not an easy character to love. He’s selfish, ambitious to a fault, and more than willing to step on people if it gets him closer to what he wants. And yet, you still root for him. That’s the magic trick of the movie. It understands that passion and ego often live side by side, and that pursuing a dream sometimes means disappointing people you care about — or choosing yourself when it hurts others.

Timothée Chalamet absolutely commits. He makes Marty feel real, flawed, and human. You don’t excuse his behavior, but you understand it. The film quietly asks how much you’re willing to sacrifice to make yourself happy — and whether the version of success you imagine is actually the one you want when you finally reach it.

Visually and tonally, the period setting never feels dusty or distant. Even though it’s rooted in another era, the energy feels modern. The ambition, the competition, the obsession — all of it translates easily to now. The world feels alive, and the culture around Marty’s pursuit is oddly infectious. You leave the theater buzzing, wanting to talk about it, replaying scenes, questioning choices.

The ending lands in a deliberately ambiguous space. It doesn’t hand you a clean emotional answer. You’re left wondering what Marty’s tears really mean — relief, regret, acceptance, or something unfinished. That uncertainty feels intentional, and it fits the story. Dreams don’t always end the way we expect them to, even when we get close.

It’s messy, driven, entertaining, and quietly reflective. A movie about ambition that doesn’t pretend ambition is noble — just human.
