Cute, unsettling, and never quite sure who it’s for.

Dust Bunny immediately signals that it’s not playing by normal rules. It’s highly stylized, surreal, and intentionally disorienting — the kind of movie where you’re not supposed to fully understand what’s happening so much as feel your way through it. Reality, imagination, and symbolism blur together almost instantly.

At its core, the movie leans on the strange relationship between the young protagonist and her odd little companion. That dynamic is where most of the charm lives. There’s something endearing there, even when the movie itself feels chaotic or childish in a way that’s hard to pin down. It almost plays like a story invented by a kid — imaginative, messy, and driven by emotion rather than logic.

That said, the tone is confusing in a way that doesn’t always feel intentional. The film feels like it might be for children, but also feels like something you absolutely shouldn’t show a child. It’s creepy without being scary, whimsical without being comforting, and strange without always being compelling. You’re left wondering who exactly the movie is made for.

There are flashes of action and creativity, and the Dust Bunny itself is undeniably cute, but the film never fully commits to being thrilling, meaningful, or emotionally grounded. It ends up feeling more like an art experiment than a fully realized story — interesting to experience, but not especially satisfying.

From the Brain

Confusing tone, unclear audience, uneven execution.

From the Heart

Weird, imaginative, mildly charming but forgettable.
