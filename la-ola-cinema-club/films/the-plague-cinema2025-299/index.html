Growing up feels scarier when you don’t know what’s real.

The Plague drops you straight into the early-2000s headspace, the kind that instantly feels familiar if you grew up around it. There’s a strong nostalgic pull, not in a comforting way, but in a disorienting one. It captures an era where everything felt slightly off, blue-tinted, and emotionally confusing. You’re back in that in-between age where the world feels big, rumors feel dangerous, and reality isn’t always clear.

What makes the movie work is how it blurs lines. You’re never totally sure how much of what you’re watching is just kids being kids, bullying, posturing, trying to fit in, versus something darker and psychological underneath. Is it a thriller? A horror story? Or just a painfully honest slice of adolescence where fear and imagination feed each other? The film refuses to answer that cleanly, and that uncertainty becomes part of the experience.

There’s a constant feeling that you’re missing something, like there are messages just out of reach, details you didn’t catch, truths forming in the background. That mirrors being young, when you rarely have the full picture but still act as if you do. Even when you think you know what’s real, the movie drops small contradictions that make you second-guess yourself.

From a character standpoint, it avoids the nostalgic fantasy of boyhood. This isn’t The Sandlot. There’s no easy brotherhood. Instead, it’s about insecurity, social pressure, and how much boys shape their behavior around what others think. You watch the main character slowly change through subtle, uncomfortable shifts until by the end he’s not quite the same kid anymore.

The Plague isn’t always easy to follow, and that feels intentional. It’s meant to feel overwhelming, confusing, and emotionally unsettled. But that discomfort is why it sticks. It captures growing up when you’re not sure if the danger is external… or something you’re creating yourself.
