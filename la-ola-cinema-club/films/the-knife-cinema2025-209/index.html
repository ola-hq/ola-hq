Truth bends in memory, in fear, in who gets to speak.

The Knife pushes into that rare space where a thriller doubles as a mirror. It’s less about what happened and more about how people tell what they think happened — how memory gaps, fear, race, and survival instincts twist the narratives that stick harder than facts. In an era when headlines often outpace reality, this film feels chillingly relevant. It captures how stories are spun — by police reports, by neighbors, by families protecting their own — and how those spins shape public belief, regardless of what’s true.

This one shook me. The ending left me questioning everything — not because the film was unclear, but because it was built to push you into that fog of perspective. Did we defend a man who was secretly guilty, or did small human mistakes spiral into a tragedy the system repackaged in its own image? The final radio call reframed the dad as a criminal — even though we never saw proof. It reminded me how easily decisions, skin color, and circumstance can be weaponized into a narrative that sticks, especially when the lens favors some stories over others.

Watching it, I reflected on memory itself — how we miss, how we omit, how we defend. Brianna pointed out details I’d ignored, and we pieced things together differently. No one sees the same story. Nobody is entirely right, nobody entirely wrong, and everyone is trying to protect a version of themselves. The Knife turned that mess into an unsettling, brutal piece of art.

Stripped of overanalysis, it’s simply a well-made, unnerving film. Realistic, tight, and much more thought-provoking than I expected. It kept me engaged from start to finish.
