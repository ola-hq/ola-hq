Brilliant, unbearable, and suffocating in the best worst way.

At its core, Lurker is an unflinching dive into the toxicity of relationships shaped by ego, insecurity, and proximity to fame. It belongs in the lineage of experimental indie films that don’t just tell a story but force you to feel it — awkward silences, suffocating pauses, and poor decisions stretched until they become unbearable. It’s a movie about how power is often held, not through talent or charisma, but through leverage, secrets, and manipulation. Watching it feels like being trapped in a room with people you’d normally escape from — and that’s exactly the point.

What makes it so effective is also what makes it excruciating. The acting is phenomenal, capturing the tedious awkwardness of group dynamics breaking down in real time. You don’t just see the protagonist make terrible choices — you feel the oxygen leave the room when he does. He’s both decent and poisonous at once, and that contradiction is what makes him so infuriating to watch.

Beneath the surface, the film carries a sharp commentary on mental health. The protagonist believes he’s fine, that his choices are justified, that he’s simply misunderstood — but what we’re really watching is someone unraveling without realizing it. That denial, and the damage it causes to himself and everyone around him, is where Lurker lands its heaviest punch. It’s a reminder that untreated issues don’t just destroy the individual, but ripple through entire communities.

For Blockbuster Wave, this was a split experience: a masterful piece of art on one hand, but one so difficult to endure that it bordered on unbearable. The balance leaves us with admiration and exhaustion in equal measure.
