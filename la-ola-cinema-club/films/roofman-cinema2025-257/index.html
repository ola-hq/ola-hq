Sometimes the getaway isn’t from the cops — it’s from yourself.

Roofman revisits the myth of the “good-hearted criminal” but grounds it in real emotion and true events. Channing Tatum delivers one of his most quietly compelling roles — a man both gifted and doomed by his own choices. The film sits somewhere between Catch Me If You Can and The Founder, yet resists glamorizing crime. Instead, it paints a portrait of a man caught between cleverness and conscience, survival and self-sabotage. It raises an uncomfortable truth: good people can still make bad decisions, and not everyone gets a chance to outgrow them.

You can’t help but root for him. You feel his desperation, his intelligence, the ache of someone trying to make something of himself — even if he bends rules to do it. By the end, when the real-life interview footage rolls, it lands deeply. You hear voices of those who loved him — the girlfriend, daughters, church members — still speaking with warmth and forgiveness. That’s when the tragedy sharpens: this wasn’t some career criminal, but a man who lost his way, yet never fully lost his humanity.

The film’s pacing drags at times once you know where it’s heading, but its handheld intensity — the shaky chases, the confined moments — keeps things tactile and real. There’s no Hollywood gloss here: just sweat, consequences, and a man stubbornly believing he might still find a clean exit. It’s sad, smart, and oddly hopeful. The kind of story that makes you wonder how many “bad guys” were just one more break away from something different.
